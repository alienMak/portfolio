RESEARCH STATEMENT // OCTOBER 2026

Measuring human cyber risk through decisions, not just completion.

A person can complete a security-awareness course, answer every question correctly, and still make a very different decision when an unexpected request arrives at the wrong moment. That gap is where my research interest began.

I work at the intersection of cybersecurity, human behavior, and socio-technical systems. I am interested in what people actually do when they encounter suspicious situations: whether they verify, hesitate, report, comply, or repeat a previous mistake—and how we can study those choices without reducing people to a single score.

The practical starting point has been VISHWAR, a social-engineering simulation and awareness platform I began developing from my cybersecurity research and capstone work. Building the platform made the research questions more specific. Capturing a choice is comparatively straightforward. Deciding what that choice means, whether the metric is valid, and whether it predicts anything beyond the scenario is much harder.

The question I keep returning to

What can repeated security decisions tell us about vulnerability and resilience that conventional training outcomes cannot?

I do not assume that a simulation score is equivalent to real-world susceptibility. A useful measurement framework must show where that inference is justified—and where it stops.

Four connected research directions

01 / LONGITUDINAL MEASUREMENT

How do decisions change across repeated exposures? I want to distinguish real behavioral improvement from familiarity with a particular scenario.

02 / LEARNING TRANSFER

Does a safer response to an email-based request transfer to voice, messaging, physical access, or another unfamiliar setting?

03 / SOCIO-TECHNICAL CONTEXT

How do organizational norms, authority cues, interface design, time pressure, and opportunities to verify shape decisions?

04 / HUMAN–AI DECISION SUPPORT

Can uncertainty-aware AI help identify useful interventions while keeping people informed and in control of the decisions being made?

How I want to study it

I am interested in controlled behavioral experiments, longitudinal study designs, usable-security methods, simulation, computational modeling, and mixed-methods analysis. A strong design should include meaningful baselines, comparable tasks, measurement checks, and an explanation of why an outcome may—or may not—generalize.

For VISHWAR, this includes examining measures such as risky-choice frequency, response time, repeat risk behavior, reporting, and variation across attack types. These measures are candidates for investigation, not validated indicators merely because the application can calculate them. Synthetic experiments can expose mathematical or implementation problems; evidence about human behavior requires appropriate participant research and ethical safeguards.

Beyond VISHWAR

I want the questions to remain useful outside the platform that led me to them. The broader goal is to understand how human and organizational security behavior emerges within complex technical environments—and how we might design evidence-based systems that make safer decisions easier without blaming individuals for systemic problems.

That is the direction I hope to develop through doctoral research: connecting cybersecurity and human factors with stronger experimental design, computational methods, and careful interpretation of evidence. I am open to adjacent problems in usable security, computational social science, socio-technical risk, and responsible AI where those methods can be useful.

Research record

I maintain a public research archive containing literature-review work, research questions, and methodological notes. My underlying VISHWAR code and exploratory engineering records are private. I separate proposed work from completed experiments and do not treat unpublished investigations as peer-reviewed evidence.
